# Biological Molecules

**Topic at a glance.** Living things are built from, and run on, a few key groups of molecules: **carbohydrates**, **fats (lipids)** and **proteins**. You need to know the **elements** they contain, their **roles**, how to **test** for them, and how large molecules are built from small "building-block" units. The topic also covers **enzymes** — the protein catalysts that make the chemistry of life fast enough — and how **temperature** and **pH** affect them. Questions commonly ask you to name elements, state roles, describe a **food test** and its colour change, or explain enzyme action and why heat or the wrong pH stops it.

---

## 3a — Elements in carbohydrates, fats and proteins

| Food group | Elements |
|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | Carbon, Hydrogen, Oxygen (C, H, O) |
| **Fats (lipids)** | Carbon, Hydrogen, Oxygen (C, H, O) |
| **Proteins** | Carbon, Hydrogen, Oxygen, **Nitrogen** (C, H, O, N) |

The element that sets **proteins** apart is **nitrogen** — carbohydrates and fats do not contain it. (Some proteins also contain a little sulfur.)

---

## 3b — Main roles in living organisms

| Food group | Main role |
|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | An **immediate source of energy** — glucose is broken down in respiration to release energy. |
| **Fats (lipids)** | **Long-term energy storage** and **insulation** (keeping the body warm); also help protect organs. |
| **Proteins** | **Growth and repair** of cells (building new tissue); also used to make enzymes, antibodies and some hormones. |

*Memory hook:* **c**arbohydrate = **c**urrent (quick) energy; **f**at = **f**uture (stored) energy + insulation; **p**rotein = **p**arts (growth and repair).

## 3c — Food tests

Learn each **reagent** and the **positive colour change**. Starch and protein are straight colour tests; the reducing-sugar and fat tests need an extra step.

![Food tests: starch with iodine, reducing sugar with Benedict's, protein with biuret, fat with ethanol.](../diagrams/biological-molecules/food-tests.png)

| Food | Test (reagent) | Positive result | If absent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Starch** | add **iodine** solution | **blue-black** | stays orange-brown |
| **Reducing sugar** (e.g. glucose) | add **Benedict's** solution and **heat** | **brick-red** precipitate | stays blue |
| **Protein** | add **biuret** solution | **violet / purple** | stays blue |
| **Fats** | dissolve in **ethanol**, then add water | **cloudy white emulsion** | stays clear |

- With Benedict's, the colour grades with how much sugar is present: blue → green → yellow → orange → **brick-red** (most). Remember to **heat** it in a water bath.
- The fat test is the **emulsion test**: fat does not dissolve in water, so it forms tiny droplets that make the liquid look milky.

## 3d — Large molecules from small building blocks

Large biological molecules are made by joining many small **basic units**:

| Large molecule | Built from (basic units) |
|---|---|
| **Starch, glycogen, cellulose** | many **glucose** molecules |
| **Proteins** (and polypeptides) | **amino acids** |
| **Fats (lipids)** | **glycerol + fatty acids** (one glycerol joined to three fatty acids) |

- Starch and glycogen are **storage** forms of glucose (in plants and animals); cellulose makes the plant **cell wall**.
- There are about **20 different amino acids**; the order in which they are joined decides which protein is made.

## 3e — Enzymes: mode of action (the lock and key model)

**Enzymes are biological catalysts** — proteins that **speed up chemical reactions** and are **not used up**, so they are reused. They work by **lowering the activation energy** (the energy needed to start a reaction).

![Lock and key: the substrate fits the active site to form an enzyme-substrate complex, the reaction happens, then products leave and the enzyme is reused.](../diagrams/biological-molecules/lock-and-key.png)

**The lock and key hypothesis:**
1. Each enzyme has an **active site** with a specific shape.
2. Only a **substrate** with a **complementary (matching) shape** fits into the active site, forming an **enzyme-substrate complex** (like a key in a lock).
3. The reaction happens — the substrate is broken down (or built up) — and the enzyme **lowers the activation energy**.
4. The **products** are released, and the **enzyme is unchanged**, ready to be used again.

**Specificity.** Because the active site has a particular shape, each enzyme works on **only one substrate (one reaction)** — this is why enzymes are **specific**.

## 3f — Effect of temperature and pH

![Temperature and pH: the rate rises to an optimum then falls as the enzyme denatures; each enzyme also has an optimum pH.](../diagrams/biological-molecules/temp-ph.png)

**Temperature.**
- As temperature rises, molecules move faster and collide more often, so the **rate increases** up to the **optimum temperature** (about **37°C** for human enzymes).
- Above the optimum the rate falls **sharply**: the heat changes the shape of the **active site**, so the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is **denatured**, and this is **permanent**.
- At **low temperature** the enzyme is **not** denatured — it is just slow (few collisions) and speeds up again when warmed.

**pH.**
- Each enzyme has an **optimum pH** at which it works fastest (for example, pepsin in the acidic stomach, salivary amylase around neutral).
- If the pH is too high or too low, the active site changes shape and the enzyme is **denatured**.

> **Denatured** means the active site has **changed shape permanently**, so the substrate can no longer fit and the reaction stops. (Denaturing is *not* the same as "killing" — enzymes are not alive.)

## Exam technique
- Food tests: always give **reagent + positive colour**, and say **heat** for Benedict's.
- For enzymes, use the key terms: **active site**, **enzyme-substrate complex**, **complementary shape**, **lowers activation energy**, **denatured**.
- When a graph falls after the optimum, say the enzyme is **denatured** and the **active site changes shape** — not that it is "killed".
- Separate **low temperature** (slows, reversible) from **high temperature** (denatures, permanent).

## Common mistakes to avoid
- Saying proteins contain only C, H, O — they also contain **nitrogen**.
- Forgetting to **heat** in the Benedict's (reducing-sugar) test.
- Saying enzymes are "used up" — they are **not**; they are reused.
- Calling a denatured enzyme "killed" — enzymes are **not alive**; the **active site changes shape**.
- Saying a substrate fits "any" enzyme — each active site is **specific** to one substrate.

## Sample questions and model answers

**How to approach these questions.** Food tests want the **reagent + the positive colour** (and "heat" for Benedict's). Enzyme answers should name the structures (**active site**, **enzyme-substrate complex**) and explain graph changes in terms of the **shape of the active site** and **denaturing**. A **(1)** marks where each mark is awarded.

**Question 1.** Describe how you would test a food sample for protein, and give the result if protein is present. **[2]**

*Answer:* Add biuret solution to the sample (1); if protein is present it turns violet/purple (1).

**Question 2.** Using the lock and key model, explain why an enzyme can break down only one type of substrate. **[3]**

*Answer:* Each enzyme has an active site with a specific shape (1); only a substrate with a complementary (matching) shape fits into it to form an enzyme-substrate complex (1); a substrate of a different shape cannot fit, so the enzyme is specific to one substrate (1).

**Question 3.** The rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction rises as temperature increases to 37°C, then falls sharply. Explain the fall. **[3]**

*Answer:* Above the optimum temperature the heat changes the shape of the enzyme's active site (1); the substrate can no longer fit, so no enzyme-substrate complex forms (1); the enzyme is denatured (permanently), so the rate falls (1).
